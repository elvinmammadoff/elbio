Design Systems · May 14, 2025 · 8 min read

Why Most Design
Systems Die
in Year Two

The failure mode isn't technical — it's organizational. Here's what actually kills design systems after the initial excitement fades, and what to do about it.

Every year, design teams celebrate launching their new design system. There's a launch post on Medium. The Figma file gets shared company-wide. The engineering team finally has a component library to reference. Leadership calls it a strategic investment.

Eighteen months later, the system is outdated. Half the components have undocumented variants. New product teams are building their own component libraries because the official one "doesn't have what we need." The designers who built it have moved on or moved sideways. And nobody is quite sure who owns it anymore.

This is not a rare outcome. Based on my experience working with design systems at eight different companies, I'd estimate it's the most common outcome.

The launch is not the product

The first mistake is treating a design system launch as a shipping event rather than the start of an ongoing operation. A design system is a product — and like any product, it requires continuous investment in three areas: engineering, documentation, and adoption.

Most teams invest heavily in the first two and neglect the third. You can have a perfectly documented, technically excellent component library that nobody uses, because adoption is a behavior change problem, not an information problem.

A design system nobody uses is just a very expensive Figma file.

Adoption requires a different kind of investment than building. It requires relationship-building with consuming teams, understanding their specific friction points, and iterating on the system to actually solve those friction points — not the ones you imagined they'd have.

The governance vacuum

The second common failure mode is what I call the governance vacuum: the system gets built by a team, but nobody is clearly designated as the decision-maker for contested changes.

This sounds like an edge case. It isn't. Every mature design system will eventually face situations where two teams need the same component to behave differently. Button variants. Modal sizes. Navigation patterns. Without clear governance, these decisions get resolved in the worst possible ways: by whoever is most persistent, by whoever has the most political capital, or — most often — by silently building a fork.

The teams I've seen handle this best treat the design system team as a platform team with an explicit RFC (Request for Comments) process borrowed from open-source software. Changes of a certain scope require a proposal, a comment period, and a decision by a defined group. It slows down some decisions — and prevents a thousand forks.

What a healthy system looks like

The healthiest design system I've worked with had four characteristics that the others lacked:

  1. Dedicated ownership. Not "the design system team" as a shared responsibility across six people. One person with design system work as their primary job description, with a clear mandate to say no.
  2. A contribution model. Any team could propose new components or changes through a lightweight process. Good ideas from the periphery could get into the system without requiring a political campaign.
  3. Versioning that teams could actually use. Major versions with migration guides. Not "we pushed an update, go check the Figma file."
  4. Metrics. Not just "number of components" — adoption rate per product team, time-to-first-use for new engineers, ratio of in-system to out-of-system components in production.

None of these require exceptional technical resources. They require treating the system like the product infrastructure it is.

The conversation worth having

If you're at a company where the design system is struggling, the right conversation to have isn't "how do we fix the Figma file." It's: who is accountable for system health, what does success look like, and what resources are we willing to commit on an ongoing basis.

The answer to that last question usually determines everything else.
